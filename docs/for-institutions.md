# Valence: information for institutional reviewers

Valence is an independent, free project made for any learner. It is not affiliated with any school and there is no vendor relationship: no contract, no account manager, and no support agreement. An institution that chooses to point students to Valence does its own review. This page gives each reviewing department the facts it needs.

Each section states only what is true of the current version. The current version has the molecule explorer, the periodic table, three labs (the standardization of a base by titration, reactions in aqueous solution, and flame tests) and one mechanism timeline: hydrochloric acid and sodium hydrogen carbonate, step by step. What the labs show about hazards, and what they do not, is under Lab safety at the end of this page.

## IT and security

- **What it is:** a static website (HTML, CSS, JavaScript). There is no server-side code, database, or API.
- **Install:** none. It has been tested in Chromium (the engine behind Chrome and Edge), where scripted checks are run against the production build. Firefox and Safari have not been tested yet.
- **Accounts:** none. There is no sign-in of any kind.
- **Network:** the app requests only its own files, from the server it was loaded from. Fonts and all data are bundled; nothing is loaded from a third-party CDN and nothing is sent anywhere. Parts of the app (the molecule pages, the 3D viewer, the periodic table, each of the two labs, the mechanism timeline and its 3D scene) and some font files are fetched from those same files the first time they are needed.
- **Storage:** preferences and learning progress are stored in the browser's `localStorage` under keys beginning `valence:`. Nothing is stored anywhere else. The titration lab stores one entry, `valence:lab:titration`, and nothing before the learner presses Start. It holds the seed of the run, the mode, what the learner did at the bench and what they typed (the state of the glassware, each trial's additions, readings and molarities), and the learner's own average and relative deviation. Results (passed or not, percent error, causes) are worked out from that each time it is read and are not stored. The entry holds nothing that identifies the learner. The file that "Export my progress" downloads holds the seed, and the values the lab hides from the learner follow from the seed for anyone who reads the source code. An entry that cannot be read, or that was written by another version of the entry's format, is set aside under a key beginning `valence:backup:` and is not deleted; the lab's page then starts fresh and says, on that visit and until the page is reloaded or a new run is started, that a saved run was kept and that the export includes it. With the lab open in two tabs of one browser, each tab takes the record the other saves at the moment it is saved, so neither goes on from an old one; nothing is sent between them but the browser's own notice that the stored entry changed. If the browser refuses to store progress, the lab page and Settings say that progress will last for that session only. The aqueous reactions lab stores one entry in the same way, `valence:lab:aqueous`, and nothing before the learner presses Start. It holds the mode, whether the lab is in progress or completed, and for each of the ten stations whether its tube has been mixed, which tools have been used, the observation as it was last checked and the equation last checked in each of the three forms. It has no seed, because the stations are fixed, and it holds nothing that identifies the learner. Whether an entry is correct is worked out each time the record is read and is not stored. What a learner has chosen or built at a station and not yet checked is kept by the page while it is open and is not stored at all: a reload loses it. A stored entry that does not fit is set aside and reported as the titration lab's is, and two tabs take each other's saved record the same way. The flame test lab stores one entry in the same way, `valence:lab:flame`, and nothing before the learner presses Start. It holds the mode, whether the lab is in progress or completed, what the loop carries now (the names of the solutions it was dipped in since it was last cleaned), the answer last given to the closing question and, for each of the six stations, what the loop carried when it was last held in the flame there, which of the flame and the strip's lines and bands have been looked at since, and the three answers last checked with what the loop carried when they were made. It has no seed, and it holds nothing that identifies the learner. Whether an observation counts and whether it is correct are worked out each time the record is read and are not stored. What a learner has chosen and not yet checked is kept by the page while it is open and is not stored. An entry that does not fit is set aside and reported in the same way, and two tabs take each other's saved record. The mechanism timeline stores one entry, `valence:mechanism:hcl-nahco3`, and nothing until the learner does something on its page: opening the page stores nothing, and neither does following a link to one of its chapters. The entry holds the mode, whether the timeline is in progress or completed, where on the timeline the learner last was (a number from 0 to 1), and the first answer given to each of its four questions, as the name of the answer chosen. Whether an answer was right is worked out each time the record is read and is not stored. The entry holds nothing that identifies the learner. It is written at each action. While the timeline plays it is written when it enters a chapter and when playing stops, the page being left among the ways it stops, and not at every frame. "Start again" on the timeline's page writes a fresh run over it, after asking. An entry that does not fit is set aside and reported in the same way.
- **Inline script:** besides its script files, the page carries one short script written into `index.html` by the build. It reads the stored theme and reduced-motion preferences and applies them before the page is first drawn, so that the dark theme does not flash light on every load. It requests nothing and stores nothing. A host whose Content Security Policy forbids inline scripts can still serve Valence: the app applies the same preferences once it has loaded, a moment later.
- **Shared computers:** on a shared browser profile, the next person can see the previous learner's saved progress. Settings has a "Clear my progress" button, and clearing site data in the browser has the same effect.
- **Size of the molecule library:** when the library went from 31 to 101 entries (2026-10-09), the list of molecules that the first page loads (names, formulas, topics) grew from 3.9 kB to 12.9 kB, and the list of sources, which the first page also loads, by 72 entries (154 to 226). Measured in Chromium on the Home page, the scripts of the first load went from 545 kB to 572 kB before compression, which is 168 kB to 171 kB as a server sends them with gzip. The structures themselves, 105 files, are not in the first load: they are in one file of 202 kB (32 kB with gzip; it was 44 kB and 7 kB), fetched when a molecule's page or the mechanism timeline is first opened. The library page lists from the small list and no longer fetches them.
- **Self-hosting:** run `npm ci && npm run build`, then serve the `dist/` folder from any static web server. No rewrite rules are needed, and it works from a sub-path.
- **Source code:** public under the MIT licence.

## Legal and privacy

- **Personal data:** Valence collects none. It has no accounts, no forms that ask for personal information, no analytics, and no cookies.
- **Data leaving the device:** none. Progress never leaves the learner's browser unless the learner uses "Export my progress" to download their own file.
- **Education records:** because nothing is collected or transmitted, Valence creates and holds no records about any student. Whether and how an institution uses Valence under FERPA or similar rules is the institution's determination; this page states the facts for that review and is not legal advice.
- **Children's privacy (COPPA) and GDPR:** the same facts apply. No personal information is collected from anyone, of any age, in any location.
- **Third-party data:** chemistry data is cited to its sources on the About page, which acknowledges PubChem (National Library of Medicine), CIAAW and NIST, and names the publishers the titration lab's constants, primer and hazard information come from. The source repository also keeps copies of the pages the periodic table was built from, as a research record; one of them, a Wikipedia page, is under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike licence and is attributed beside the copy. The copies are not part of the built app. For the titration lab the repository keeps four copies (two Wikipedia revisions, attributed beside them, and one data file each from NOAA and PubChem). Every other page the lab cites, the safety data sheets among them, was read and is not copied: the research record says what each was read to print, and where. For the aqueous reactions lab the repository keeps one copy, a data file from PubChem; its other 30 sources and its 25 safety data sheets were read and are not kept. For the mechanism timeline the repository keeps no copy of any source.
- **No quotation in the titration lab's text.** The lab's primer, its hazard notes and its messages are written in Valence's own words. Two things are stored as their sources print them, because they are data: the digits of a cited figure, and a hazard statement with its code. A test holds the primer, the hazard notes and the research record's rows for the safety data sheets to repeating no run of six words of the pages they rest on. That test needs a fingerprint of those pages which is not kept in the repository, because stretches of the pages could be rebuilt from it. So the test runs only on a machine where the fingerprint has been made, and is reported as skipped everywhere else, the automated build on the code host included. It was run when the words were written; nothing re-runs it for every change. The aqueous reactions lab has a test of the same kind over its primer, its stations' notes and hints and its two cautions, which runs, and is skipped, on the same terms; its reagents' hazard notes are not under that test. The sentences written into that lab's screens (headings, button names, refusals) are Valence's own and rest on no page. The mechanism timeline has a test of the same kind over its chapters' text, its questions with their answers and feedback, its notes and what it says of each energy, on the same terms. The pages it is compared against are the paper its acid constant is from, two abstracts that were read as a check, and a listing of constants; none of them is kept. The tables of 1982 are a scan with no text to compare against. The flame test lab has a test of the same kind over its primer, its stations' notes, what they say the strip leaves out, the reasons stored with the model's own choices and the research record's rows, on the same terms; it was run, with the fingerprint, on 2026-10-09. The six bottles' hazard notes are not under that test. A script that is not in the repository compared the six with the text of the documents they rest on when one of them was changed on 2026-10-08, and found no run of six words repeated.
- **Licence:** MIT.

## Accessibility services

Valence has not had an accessibility audit, and this page makes no claim of conformance to WCAG or any other standard. This section lists what was built, how it was checked, and what has not been checked.

**How it was checked.** Scripts drove the production build in headless Chromium 153 (the engine behind Chrome and Edge) on Linux, using the keyboard only where the check is about the keyboard. Screenshots were taken in the light and dark themes at 1366×768 and 400×800 (the periodic table also at 2523×1290) and looked at by the AI coding assistant that built the screens; no person has reviewed them yet. The chemistry text (descriptions, names, measurements) comes from functions with automated tests.

**What was checked that way:**

- **Keyboard operation of the explorer.** With no mouse: reach the library from the menu, open a molecule, switch between the four views, turn and zoom the 3D model with the camera buttons, open the text description, select atoms and bonds from the Atoms and bonds list, open the "More about" and "Sources" parts of the cards, measure a distance and an angle, clear the selection, change the valence display level, switch between the neutral and zwitterion forms, and jump to another molecule with the search dialog (Ctrl+K, ⌘K, or the Search button in the menu). Focus moves to the page heading after each navigation (the periodic table has one exception, described next), returns to where it was when the search dialog is closed with Escape, and has a visible indicator on every stop in the molecule page's tab order.
- **The library's filter (added 2026-10-09, checked at 820×1180 and 1366×768, light theme).** The library page lists 101 molecules, so it has a search field labelled "Filter molecules" above the list. Typing a name or a formula narrows the list as it is typed; a status line under the field gives the count ("11 of 101 molecules") or says that nothing matches; a Clear button appears once something is typed, and Escape clears the field too. Both leave focus in the field. From the field, with the keyboard only: "caff" was typed, Tab pressed twice (Clear, then the one molecule left) and Enter opened it. The script put focus in the field itself; that Tab reaches the field from the top of the page was not tried, though it is an ordinary input in the page's order. A group with no match is left out, heading and all. Whether a screen reader reads the status line as it changes was not tested. On an element's card, a list of more than twelve molecules is folded under its count ("71 molecules of the library contain carbon"); it was opened with Enter on the card of carbon at 820×1180, with focus put on it by the script.
- **Keyboard operation of the periodic table.** With no mouse: reach the table from the library page, from Home, or from the search dialog; move between the elements; open an element and read its card; change the colouring and the highlight; open the list of all elements; jump to another element or to a molecule with the search dialog; and follow the link on a molecule's atom card to that element. The table is drawn as two grids, the main table and the two rows below it, which together are a single stop in the tab order. Inside them the arrow keys move to the nearest element in that direction, across the gap between the two grids; Home and End go to the ends of a row, and Ctrl+Home and Ctrl+End to the first and last element of the table; Enter or Space opens the element. An arrow key at an edge does nothing, and does not scroll the page. When focus leaves the grids, the stop stays on the selected element, or on the cell that last had focus when none is selected. Colouring and highlighting are two ordinary drop-down lists, and each chemical family in the legend is a toggle button. Every stop on the page has a visible focus indicator.
- **The table's exception to the focus rule.** Opening an element from the table, or from the list of all elements, leaves focus on the cell or link that was chosen, so the arrow keys can go on from there; a status line announces "Selected: Fluorine. Its details are below the table." The highlighted set is announced in a second status line, so that choosing an element does not read the highlight out again. A "Clear selection" link follows the table, and the element's card follows that. Reaching an element any other way (the search dialog, the link on an atom card, or the browser's Back button from another page) moves focus to the page heading as everywhere else. Opening elements one after another does not add to the browser history, so Back leaves the table in one step.
- **Names and alternatives in the periodic table.** Each cell's name gives the element's name, symbol, atomic number and chemical family, the value and unit when the table is coloured by a property ("no value listed" when there is none), and whether it is highlighted. The columns and rows of the main table have group and period headers. The cells of lanthanum and actinium are also described by a sentence saying that cerium to lutetium, or thorium to lawrencium, come next and are drawn below the main table. Below the table, "All 118 elements as a list" gives every element as a plain link in atomic-number order with the same value beside it, so a trend can be read without the grid or its colours. The shell diagram on an element's card is hidden from assistive technology and its electron counts are given as text. Every link, button, drop-down list and disclosure on the page has a text name.
- **Text alternatives.** The 3D model and the 2D Lewis drawing are each exposed as one image with a name ("3D ball and stick model of Water") and a description: the formula, the number of atoms and bonds, each central atom's geometry, and where the lone pairs are. The same description can be read on the page under "Text description". Every atom and bond is in the Atoms and bonds list by name, and the current selection and any measurement are written in a status line.
- **Reduced motion.** With the operating system set to reduce motion, or with Reduced motion set to On in Settings, the 3D model stops as soon as a drag is released; with motion allowed it coasts briefly. Nothing in the explorer moves by itself in either case. On the periodic table's page nothing moves. The only transition is the brief fade of the focus ring on the legend's buttons, as on buttons elsewhere, and it is off when motion is reduced.
- **Theme at load.** The light or dark theme (the learner's choice in Settings, or the operating system's) is in place before the page is first drawn. With the system set to light and the dark theme chosen, and the reverse, every frame Chromium painted from the first was in the chosen theme, on Home, Settings and the periodic table at 1366×768; so it was with nothing stored, with a stored value that was corrupt, and with storage blocked, where the system's theme is used. With storage that could be read but refused every write, the stored theme was in place in every frame sampled on Settings, in both directions, and Settings said that nothing is being saved. The browser is told which theme is in use (`color-scheme`), which is what it draws its own parts by; the drop-down lists and the scrollbars themselves were not looked at.
- **Colour is not the only signal.** A selected atom or bond has a tick in the list and a ring in the model, and is named in the status line; the current view and form have a tick. Elements are coloured in the model, and each atom's element is also in its name ("Oxygen 1") and on its card. In the periodic table the chemical family is named in the legend and in each cell's name as well as coloured; a highlighted set has a solid border and the rest lose their fill and take a dashed border, with no fading, so the text keeps its full contrast; a property's value is printed in the cell as well as shaded; an element with no value is hatched and named "no value listed"; the selected element has a double border.
- **Fit.** At 1366×768 and at 400×800 every one of the 118 elements was opened and the page never scrolled sideways. When the table does not fit at its smallest cell size (44 pixels, or 56 while a property's values are shown), it scrolls sideways inside its own box. At 400×800 every cell reached with the arrow keys was wholly inside that box and the window, with its focus outline. The name and value of the focused or pointed-at element are written in a strip under the box, which does not scroll sideways with the table; it follows whichever was used last, naming the focused element after a key press and the pointed-at element after the pointer moves. When a cell takes focus from the keyboard, the page scrolls as far as it must, at once and without animation, to bring that strip into the window with the cell: at 400×800 and at 360×640 the strip was in the window and named the focused element after Tab into the table and after every key press that followed, with and without a property's values shown. A click or a tap scrolls nothing: at 400×800 and at 1366×768 a cell half cut off by the edge of the box or of the window opened on the first click. The first key pressed after such a click (Enter, Space, an arrow or a letter) brings the cell and its outline wholly into view. A modifier pressed on its own (Shift, Control, Alt or the Meta key) scrolls nothing, so that a Shift+click opens the cell under the pointer; Shift alone does make Chromium draw the outline, which is then cut by the edge until the next key. An element opened from the search, the list or its address has its cell scrolled into view.
- **Names.** Every button, link, field and menu on the molecule page, with the search dialog open, has a text name.
- **Without WebGL.** If the browser cannot start the 3D model, the page says so, and the 2D Lewis view, the list, the cards and the text description still work.

**What has not been checked:**

- No person has tested Valence with a screen reader. The roles, names and descriptions above were checked by script, not by listening to them.
- No browser other than Chromium: not Firefox, not Safari, and no phone or tablet browser. Touch has not been tested.
- No person with a colour-vision deficiency has looked at the periodic table. Its ten family colours were chosen by a script that simulates three kinds of colour blindness and keeps the colours as far apart as it can; that is a simulation, not a test with people. Two pairs of families (transition metals and noble gases; post-transition metals and noble gases) are the closest even for full colour vision.
- Text contrast has not been measured on the rendered pages, with one exception: the text of the periodic table's cells against their fill, measured by script in Chromium at 1366×768 and 400×800. It is at least 9.64:1 on the family colours in the light theme and 7.01:1 in the dark theme, and at least 7.96:1 (light) and 6.56:1 (dark) on the property shading.
- Browser zoom and enlarged text have not been tested. Forced-colour (high contrast) mode was tried for the periodic table only, in Chromium's emulation and not with a Windows contrast theme: the borders that mark selection, highlight and focus remain when the colours are removed, and so does the hatching of an element with no value. The family colours and the property shading do not: there the family is given only by the names in the legend and in each cell's name, and a property only by the printed values.

**Known limits:**

- Atoms cannot be reached one by one with the keyboard inside the 3D model or the Lewis drawing. The Atoms and bonds list beside them is the keyboard path and selects the same things.
- On a canvas, dragging turns the model and the mouse wheel zooms it instead of scrolling the page.
- Some names contain subscript characters ("Oxygen (O₂)"), which a screen reader may read oddly.
- How a screen reader announces the periodic table is not known: whether it says the group and period headers, and what it says on crossing from the main table to the two rows below it, were not heard.
- An element's card is below the table. On most screens it is out of view when an element is chosen; the status line says where it is, and so does the readout in the table's empty corner.
- In a cell, an element's name is shown only on a wide screen, and a value with more digits than fit in the cell is printed there rounded to the digits that fit, not cut short: at 1366×768 and below that is 13 ionization energies and hydrogen's density, in type 9 pixels high. The selected cell, whose border is wider, prints one digit fewer at some widths. The legend says that cells round. The full name and value are in the cell's accessible name, in the readout (above the transition metals, or under the table where the table scrolls sideways), in the list of all elements, and on the element's card. The readout itself is hidden from assistive technology because it repeats the cell's name.
- A cell is laid out for the type sizes above. With a minimum font size set in the browser (Chromium's setting was tried, at 400 and 1366 pixels wide), nothing was cut at 12 pixels. At 16 pixels the three lines of a cell no longer fit its height and the lower part of each value's digits is cut off; the two values too wide for the cell even when rounded, the densities of hydrogen and helium, are cut as well, and the mark that would show it falls in the part of the line that is cut off. At 20 pixels the values are pushed out of the cells and are not seen at all. The places listed in the item above still give the value whole.
- Once an element is selected, the table is re-entered at that element, not at the cell that last had focus.
- Where the table scrolls sideways, the readout strip under it is not always in the window. From the keyboard it is brought there only if the window is tall enough for the table and the strip together (566 pixels at 400 wide, and up to 604 while a property's values are shown); in a shorter window (400×500 was tried) the focused cell is kept in view and the strip is cut off or below the window's edge while a cell in the top two rows has focus. With a mouse or a touch screen nothing scrolls: at 400×800 with the page at its top the strip is below the window's edge, and the learner has to scroll down about 300 pixels to have it in view with the whole table. Until then the cell shows only the symbol and atomic number. The name is still in the cell's accessible name, for assistive technology, and once the element is opened in the status line, which is under the strip and so no nearer.
- The search dialog lists 159 entries when nothing has been typed.

### The titration lab: accessibility

As above, there has been no audit and this is no claim of conformance.

**How it was checked, and by whom.** The lab was built and checked by the AI coding assistant that wrote it. Its scripts drove the lab's page in headless Chromium 153 on Linux with the keyboard alone (Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space, the arrow keys, Escape and typing; no pointer, and focus was never set by the script), on the development server and on the production build, in the light and dark themes. Before the page was whole, the parts of the bench were driven the same way one at a time, outside the app. The scripts are not part of the repository. No person took part in these checks. How the lab looks (colour, spacing, proportion, how fast the stream runs, how pink the flask is) is for the project's author to judge by eye and is not reported here.

**What was checked that way:**

- **A whole lab by keyboard.** One complete Guided lab, with three trials that count and the learner's average and relative deviation entered, and one Challenge trial from Start to its result. Every control of the lab is a button, a radio button, a text field, a disclosure or a link, reached with Tab and worked with Enter, Space or, for the two modes, the arrow keys. No action needs a pointer, a held key or timing. A key held down adds one drop or one half-drop. The stream is a toggle: it is closed by the same key on the same button, or by Escape wherever focus is, and "Run in 1 mL" and "Run in 5 mL" deliver that volume and close by themselves.
- **The page's three tabs.** The lab's page is in three tabs at every width: Primer, Lab (the Guided checklist, the bench and the notebook) and Record (the table of the run's trials). The tab list takes one stop in the tab order; the arrow keys move between the tabs and wrap round, and Enter or Space shows the one focus is on. Each tab names its panel. A panel that is not shown is hidden and inert: Tab reached nothing in it, and it is kept in the page, so that what was typed in a field, the bench's message line and a warning that is showing were all still there after a visit to another tab. The selected tab is marked by an underline and a heavier weight as well as by its state. The bar with the tabs stays at the top of the window while a panel scrolls, and what takes focus in a panel comes to rest below the bar, not behind it. A tab that was left with its panel scrolled under the bar came back at the same place: with the stopcock in view on the Lab tab, a visit to the Primer tab and back left the stopcock's button where it had been, at 1024×768, at 820×1180 by touch and at 1366×768, by pointer and by keyboard. The page opens on the Primer tab when there is no run and on the Lab tab when there is one; which tab is shown, and where each was left, is not stored and is not in the address.
- **A stream is not left running unseen.** With the stopcock open, choosing another tab closes it: nothing was delivered while the other tab was shown, and back on the Lab tab the message line read "Stopcock closed: it was closed when you left the Lab tab."
- **The primer, one topic at a time.** The Primer tab shows one of the twelve topics as a card, with "Topic 7 of 12" over its heading, and under it buttons named for the topics either side ("Previous: Why phenolphthalein", "Next: The calculation"); at the first topic there is no Previous and at the last no Next. A list of the twelve topics, in a navigation region named "Topics", marks the one shown (with a bar and a heavier weight, and for a screen reader as current) and puts the words "Read first" on the four the primer says to read before the bench. Where the primer is wide enough the list is beside the card; where it is not, it is behind a button named "Topics" that says whether the list is out. Choosing a topic by any of these moves focus to the card's heading.
- **Focus.** A Guided warning takes focus when it appears, and after either of its two buttons focus returns to the control that raised it. When a trial ends, focus moves to its result in the notebook; "Start the next trial" moves it to the bench; Start shows the Lab tab and moves focus to its panel, which is named "Lab", with the tab bar at the top of the window and the checklist's line and the top of the shelf under it, whether Start was pressed above the tabs or on the Lab tab, by keyboard, pointer or touch. All of these are inside the Lab tab, and none of them happened while another tab was shown. At 1366×768 and at 2523×1290 a warning was wholly in view once it had focus.
- **Controls that cannot be used yet.** A bench button that cannot be used yet stays in the tab order and is marked unavailable. Pressing it says why in the bench's message line, in words that name what is missing and nothing else. The message line is a status region, which a screen reader speaks when its text changes, so a sentence that is already showing there is said the second time with "Again:" before it: a second drop of indicator, a second swirl that finds the same, or a second press refused for the same reason then changes the text as the first did. The line is emptied when the notebook or a recorded reading has moved the trial on. A field that cannot be used yet is disabled, with its reason shown under it and tied to it. The two exceptions are under Known limits.
- **Steps done, and entries made.** (Added on 2026-10-06, at the author's request after using the lab on a tablet.) A step of the shelf that is done once is one button: it is named for the action until the step is done, and for what is so afterwards ("Burette conditioned", "Pipette wet with HCl", "HCl pipetted into the flask"), with a tick in front that is hidden from assistive technology, so the words are the button's name. Such a button is marked unavailable, stays in the tab order and says why when pressed. The fill's button reads "Burette filled. Refill" once the burette is filled, and still refills it whenever a fill is allowed; once the burette has run out to its last mark it reads "Fill burette" again, with no tick. A field whose entry has been made (the HCl molarity, a burette reading, the NaOH molarity, the learner's average and relative deviation) is disabled and shows the entry. Where that entry can still be made again, the button beside the field reads "Change" and is described by the field's label, because the Tab key passes over a disabled field. Change puts focus in the field with the old entry selected, "Cancel", or the Escape key anywhere in the field's form, puts the field back, and the old entry stands until a new one is recorded. A held Enter key counts as one press. When Enter records an entry, focus moves from the field, which is then disabled, to that button; it did so after a Guided warning too. Where no entry can be made, a trial that is over among them, the field has its one reason under it and nothing else. These were driven by script in headless Chromium on the production build, in the dark theme: 72 checks, all passing. The script put focus on a control and then used the keyboard; two buttons were pressed by pointer. The Escape key, the held Enter key and the fill's name at the last mark were added after a check by a reviewer who had not built these, and were driven the same way in both themes.
- **Text for the drawings.** The drawing of the burette and flask and the enlarged view of the scale are each one image with a name and a description, and each description can also be read on the page. "The meniscus in words" gives the two graduations the meniscus lies between and which fifth of the gap it is in, and never the reading itself; it is offered to every learner. In the scripted lab every burette reading was taken from those words alone, and every one was accepted. The description of the stand gives the level to the whole millilitre, the stopcock's position, an air bubble in the tip, and what is in the flask and its colour. In every state the scripts reached, the words agreed with the state of the drawing.
- **Names.** Every button, link, field, disclosure, group and table on the lab's page has a text name, before Start, with the bench shown and with a warning showing.

**What exists.** The molecule explorer, the periodic table, three labs (the standardization of a base by titration, described under "The titration lab" below; reactions in aqueous solution, under "The aqueous reactions lab" after it; and flame tests, under "The flame test lab" after that) and one mechanism timeline, under "The mechanism timeline" after those.

- Four views: ball and stick, space filling (van der Waals radii), wireframe, and a 2D Lewis structure with lone pairs and formal charges.

**Conventions chosen.** Where textbooks differ, Valence follows a general chemistry text (OpenStax Chemistry 2e) and notes the convention on the card where it applies:

- **Hybridization** comes from the steric number. The O–H and ester oxygens of acids and esters are sp³ and bent; their card adds that organic chemistry describes them as sp², because one lone pair is delocalised into the neighbouring C=O. The oxygen of a phenol or of an aryl ether is treated the same way, and its card names the aromatic ring. A nitrogen with a lone pair next to a double bond of carbon is the one exception: the nitrogen of an amide or of urea, and the N–H of a ring such as that of serotonin or of indigo, is shown as sp² and trigonal planar, as its model is.
- **Terminal atoms.** Hydrogen reads "none (1s)". A terminal atom with a double or triple bond shows a value (a carbonyl oxygen is sp²). Singly bonded halogens and O⁻ read "not assigned (terminal atom)" and get no VSEPR geometry.
- **Expanded octets.** Steric numbers 5 and 6 read "sp³d (textbook label)" and "sp³d² (textbook label)", with the note that real d-orbital involvement is negligible. No lobes are drawn for them.
- **Sulfur dioxide** is drawn in the octet-obeying resonance form: one S=O, one S–O, formal charges +1 on sulfur and −1 on one oxygen. Its bond cards say this is one of two equivalent structures.
- **Sulfate, phosphate, sulfuric acid, phosphoric acid and sulfur trioxide** are drawn the same way, in the octet-obeying form: single bonds from sulfur or phosphorus to every oxygen (one S=O in sulfur trioxide), a formal charge of +2 on sulfur or +1 on phosphorus, and −1 on each oxygen that carries no hydrogen. Many texts, and PubChem's records, draw S=O and P=O double bonds instead, which lowers the formal charges and gives the central atom more than eight electrons. Oxidation states, geometry and hybridization are the same in both drawings. The page of each of the five says that both drawings are in use.
- **Resonance elsewhere.** The carboxylate of each zwitterion and of the acetate ion, the two unprotonated C–O bonds of bicarbonate, the two O–O bonds of ozone and the two N–O bonds of nitric acid to the oxygens that carry no hydrogen are drawn as one structure of two, and read as order 1.5. The three bonds of nitrate, of carbonate and of sulfur trioxide are drawn as one structure of three, and read as order 1.33. The bonds of a benzene ring are shown as aromatic, order 1.5, wherever one occurs; a ring that holds nitrogen is not (see the known limits).
- **Oxidation state** gives each bond's electrons to the more electronegative atom (Pauling) and splits them between atoms of the same element. Every atom of an uncharged element (H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, ozone) is 0.
- **Geometry.** Nineteen small molecules use experimental gas-phase geometry. The rest use one computed conformer; ethanol, glucose and ethyl acetate carry the note "one conformer of many", and so does every molecule added later for which PubChem lists more than one conformer.

**Known limits.**

- The covalent, van der Waals and ionic radii come from paywalled papers and were checked only against secondary transcriptions that agree with each other.
- The xenon electronegativity (2.6) has no traced primary source. The atom card says so under the value.
- PubChem 3D structures are computed conformers, not measurements. The bicarbonate conformer shows its two equivalent C–O bonds at different lengths; its page says so. The 19 molecules added to the basics and VSEPR groups that use a computed conformer (hydrogen fluoride, chlorine, hydrogen sulfide, the acids, the ions and others) were not compared with measured structures; the first 13 of those groups are all experimental.
- **Rings that hold nitrogen are not marked aromatic.** The pyridine ring of nicotine, the rings of adenine, thymine, caffeine and theobromine, and the five-membered ring of serotonin and of indigo are stored as PubChem draws them, with their double bonds where they are drawn. "Order 1.5" is the textbook statement for a benzene ring and not for these, so the library says nothing of their aromaticity.
- **Three molecules that were asked for are not in the library.** Beryllium chloride and bromine pentafluoride: beryllium and bromine are not among the 11 elements of the explorer's element table, and adding an element takes values from sources that were not read for them (the radii above). Without bromine pentafluoride the library has no square pyramidal molecule. The triiodide ion: NIST's database has no experimental geometry for it, and iodine is not in the element table either.
- **The sentence under an everyday molecule says only what its PubChem record says.** Geosmin "has an earthy smell" (nothing read says it is the smell of rain); indigo is "a dye" (nothing read says it is blue). The record whose structure is used for indigo (CID 5318432) has no description; the sentence rests on PubChem's main record for indigo (CID 10215), which draws a tautomer, and says so.
- **The zwitterion note on serine and cysteine** ("the form found in water and in the solid") is the sentence the spec gives every zwitterion. It was not read again for these two.
- The hydrogen peroxide H–O–O angle of 94.8° is the 1962 measurement NIST lists; later measurements give about 100–102°. Its page says so.
- Hybrid lobes are a simplified shape drawn along each bond and lone-pair direction. The lone pair of an amide or urea nitrogen is described in a note and not drawn as a lobe.
- The molecular orbital diagrams show the order of the levels, not their energies.
- The chemistry has been checked against the cited sources by script and by a second pass over the research. It has not been reviewed by a chemistry instructor.

### The periodic table

**What it shows.** All 118 elements in the 18-column layout, with cerium to lutetium and thorium to lawrencium in two rows below. The table can be coloured by chemical family or by one of five properties (electronegativity, covalent radius, first ionization energy, melting point, density), shaded on a linear scale from the lowest to the highest value, with each value printed in its cell. A group, a period, a block or a family can be highlighted, and each group, period and block has a short description of what its members share. Choosing an element shows a card with its atomic mass, group, period, block, family, electron configuration, a shell diagram with the electrons per shell, electronegativity, covalent and van der Waals radii, first ionization energy, electron affinity, common oxidation states, state at 25 °C, melting point, boiling point, density, year discovered, links to the library molecules that contain it, an emission spectrum for the 97 elements that have one (and for each of the other 21 a sentence that says what NIST lists for it), and its sources. Each atom card in the molecule explorer links to its element.

**Cross-check.** A script compares the saved sources with each other and writes a report (`docs/research/periodic-table/cross-check.md`). Its outcome decided which source is shown:

- **Atomic weight.** PubChem's column agrees with CIAAW for 80 of 84 elements; argon and iron are rounding ties; lithium (7.0 against 6.94) and sulfur (32.07 against 32.06) differ. CIAAW's values are shown.
- **First ionization energy.** PubChem's column agrees with NIST for 76 of the 102 elements both list, and differs for 26, nine of them by 0.1 eV or more. NIST's values are shown.
- **Electron configuration.** PubChem agrees with NIST's ground state for 107 of 108 elements. Lawrencium differs; PubChem's is shown, with a note giving NIST's. NIST has no entry for elements 109 to 118.
- **Electronegativity, melting point, boiling point, density, van der Waals radius.** Compared once, with Wikipedia's transcriptions of handbooks and not with a primary source. The values that stood far from the other tabulations are shown as PubChem has them, each with a note on the card.
- **Electron affinity, covalent radius, oxidation states, year discovered.** Not compared value by value with an independent source.

The report lists 41 values as unresolved: disagreements no decision settles, and values PubChem gives as plain facts that are predictions, estimates or untraced. A test fails if any of them has no note on its card.

**Notes.** A value that needs a caution carries a sentence beside it on the card, with its own source: 107 notes on 57 elements. Among them: helium's melting point (it is solid only under pressure); carbon's and arsenic's melting and boiling points (both elements turn from solid straight to gas at ordinary pressure); the density of each gas (it is for the gas at 0 °C and atmospheric pressure, which PubChem does not state and which was worked out from the values); the years for aluminum, calcium, fluorine and silicon, which are not the years those elements were first isolated; the oxidation states of krypton, xenon and radon (PubChem's list shows only 0, although compounds such as XeF₄ exist); the melting points, states and masses of the heaviest elements that are estimates or predictions; and the eight covalent radii that PubChem's pages give with no uncertainty, among them helium's and francium's, the two ends of that colour scale.

**Conventions chosen.**

- **Group 3 and the f block.** Lanthanum and actinium are in group 3 and the d block; the f block is cerium to lutetium and thorium to lawrencium, 14 in each row, with no group number. PubChem puts lutetium and lawrencium in group 3; a provisional report of an IUPAC task group in 2021 leaned the same way, and IUPAC has not ruled. The page says so in a note on group 3. Helium is in group 18 and in the s block.
- **Chemical families.** PubChem's ten categories are used as given, under the heading "Chemical family (PubChem's grouping)". - **Atomic mass has two kinds.** An element with a standard atomic weight shows it. Bismuth, thorium, protactinium and uranium have a standard atomic weight.
- **"No value listed".** A value its source leaves empty is shown with those words, never as zero and never as "not measured", because an empty cell in these sources does not always mean that.
- **Predicted and expected.** A configuration PubChem marks as predicted or calculated keeps that word, on the card and on the shell diagram; a state it words as expected is shown as expected.
- **Radius.** The table is coloured by covalent radius, which shows the trend a general chemistry course teaches. PubChem's own "atomic radius" column is a van der Waals radius from mixed sources; it is on the card under that name and is not used for colouring.
- **Electron affinity** is given as the energy released when an atom gains an electron, so a positive value means energy is released; the card says that some textbooks use the opposite sign.
- **Oxidation states** are headed "Common oxidation states (PubChem's list, not complete)". **The state** is headed "State at room temperature (25 °C)", because that is what PubChem's column holds; it is not the thermodynamic standard state. **The year** is headed "Year discovered (as PubChem lists it)".
- **Names** are spelled as PubChem spells them (aluminum, cesium). The search also finds "aluminium" and "caesium".
- **The 11 elements the explorer also describes.** Atomic weight, first ionization energy and covalent radius come from the same sources in both places. The van der Waals radii of hydrogen and fluorine differ; the element card shows the table's value and gives the explorer's value and its source in one sentence.

**Emission spectra.** Added on 2026-10-08. **Known limits of the periodic table.**

Caesium shows three red lines, because NIST gives no intensity to its lines in the blue. No line outside 400 to 700 nm is shown. Ties are not cut, so plutonium shows 49 lines. ### The titration lab

**What it teaches.** Standardizing a sodium hydroxide solution of about 0.1 M against 25.00 mL of hydrochloric acid whose molarity is on the bottle's label, with phenolphthalein as the indicator, on a drawn bench. The learner records the label's molarity, conditions the burette, fills it, clears the air bubble from its tip, reads the meniscus to 0.01 mL, rinses a pipette with the acid and delivers the aliquot, adds 2 or 3 drops of indicator, titrates to a faint pink that lasts, reads the burette again, and calculates the base's molarity as the acid's molarity × 25.00 mL ÷ the titre. Trials are repeated until three count. The learner then enters their own average and relative deviation and sees the notebook's beside them.

**The primer.** Twelve cards, written for a reader with no chemistry background: acids, bases and neutralisation; molarity; what a titration is; why sodium hydroxide has to be standardized; equivalence point and endpoint; why phenolphthalein; titrating to the endpoint; the calculation; reading the burette; conditioning, rinsing and the tip bubble; how closely the trials agree; safety. The first card names four to read before going to the bench. The cards are shown one at a time, with a list of the twelve and buttons to the one before and the one after, and those four are marked "Read first". So that a topic is quick to find inside a card, each paragraph has a short bold label at its head, each term is bold where it is defined, and each sentence that states a rule of the simulation and not of a real bench, and says so itself, is set off by a rule down its side. These were added on 2026-10-06 over the wording the author approved, which a test holds unchanged, byte for byte. Each card lists its sources, and the sources of the model's constants are under the card. The primer quotes none of its sources: beside each paragraph the source code stores where the passage it rests on is, and what that passage establishes, in Valence's own words.

**How a run is made.** Pressing Start draws a seed, which is shown in the notebook. The seed fixes the molarity on the acid bottle's label (0.0850 to 0.1050 M, to four decimal places, and exact), the molarity of the base, which the learner is not shown (0.09500 to 0.10500 M), and for each trial what the pipette delivers (25.00 mL give or take up to 0.03 mL; the learner calculates with 25.00) and where a filled burette's meniscus starts (between 0.00 and 2.00 mL). Nothing else varies. The same seed and the same actions always give the same readings and the same answer. Restart asks first and then draws a new seed.

**The bench.** The burette is graduated every 0.1 mL and is read from an enlarged view of the scale, with the eye level with the meniscus. The stopcock gives a half-drop (0.025 mL), a drop (0.05 mL), a stream (0.25 mL every 0.4 seconds) and two measured pours, of 1 mL and of 5 mL. It stays shut until the burette is filled, the flask holds the acid and 2 or 3 drops of indicator, and an initial reading is recorded. After each addition the bench says, in words as well as in the drawing, whether the pink where the drop landed fades at once, fades slowly or persists. The final reading can be recorded only after "Swirl and wait 30 s" has shown the pink to last; the 30 seconds are simulated and there is no real wait. The flask wall can be rinsed down with distilled water, 10 mL at a time and at most three times in a trial. A flask can be discarded and the trial begun again. The bench never states how much has been delivered. The description of the burette and flask gives the burette's level to the whole millilitre and no finer, and the status line and the message under the stand give no level of their own. The level is read in the enlarged view, and that view's text alternative, which every learner can open, gives the two 0.1 mL marks the meniscus lies between and which fifth of the gap it is in. The only readings printed as numbers are the ones the learner typed, and the titre worked from them. Additions are exact, though, so a learner who counts them can work out the titre without reading the burette; that is accepted.

**Two modes.** Guided shows a checklist of the steps. It warns before the burette is filled without conditioning, before an initial reading is taken with the bubble still in the tip (and of an unconditioned burette once more there), and before the acid is pipetted with an unrinsed pipette; the learner can go back or go on. It does not record a burette reading that is more than 0.02 mL from the meniscus, or an acid molarity that is not the label's, and asks for it again. Within 1.00 mL of the flask's equivalence point it shows a hint to slow down, and closes a running stream the first time the hint shows. Once both readings are in, it sets out the calculation with the learner's own numbers and stops before the answer. It never shows the expected titre, or a molarity the learner did not type. Challenge shows none of these: skipped steps, misread burettes and a miscopied label carry into the result and are named afterwards. The mode is chosen before Start and can be changed between trials, not during one. No trial is labelled with a mode.

**How answers are checked.**

- **The form of what is typed.** A burette reading is typed to exactly two decimal places and a molarity to exactly four. Anything else is refused, in both modes, and so is a comma as the decimal mark.
- **The arithmetic.** The base's molarity is accepted only when it is the learner's own recorded acid molarity × 25.00 mL ÷ their own titre, rounded to the fourth decimal place. At an exact tie, where the fifth decimal place is a 5 with nothing after it, either neighbour is accepted, because rounding a half up and rounding a half to even are both taught. A value cut off after the fourth place instead of rounded is refused, with a sentence that says to round. The check uses only numbers the learner typed, so it gives nothing away, and a refusal marks nothing.
- **The grade.** A trial passes when the molarity is within 1.0% of the base's true molarity, the edge included. A trial that does not pass shows its percent error, with its sign, and names what went wrong. A trial that passes shows no percent error.
- **Invalid trials.** A trial is invalid, and is repeated, when more than 0.10 mL of base (two drops) was added past the flask's own equivalence point; when base has run into the flask and the burette is at its 50.00 mL mark with the flask not pink; or when the learner discards the flask.
- **Three trials must count.** A trial that passes counts only when both typed burette readings were within 0.02 mL of the meniscus, the recorded acid molarity is the label's, and no technique error is named in it (burette not conditioned, tip bubble, pipette not rinsed).
- **The percent error.** The percent error of a failed trial lets a learner work out the base's true molarity. That is why a trial counts only on those three conditions. A result reached by typing a reading the burette does not show is marked "Passed, not counted", and so is one that is close only because two mistakes cancelled or because what was left of a bubble was small. Its feedback says why.
- **Completion.** When three trials count, the base's true molarity is shown, to five decimal places, and no further trial is run. The learner enters their own average and relative deviation over those three; the notebook then shows its own beside them. Neither is marked right or wrong.
- **Relative deviation** is the mean absolute deviation of the three molarities divided by their mean, in parts per thousand. The notebook works it from the average rounded to four decimal places and shows it to one decimal place. Molarities are typed to four decimal places, and one unit in the fourth place is about one part per thousand of the result, so the relative deviation of three careful trials can take only a few values and reflects rounding as much as technique.

**Technique errors are larger than at a bench, and named.** In the model, a burette filled without conditioning holds base diluted by 2.0%. An air bubble of 0.60 mL forms in the tip when the burette is filled from empty; left there, it escapes during the first 5 mL delivered, so the reading falls 0.60 mL more than went into the flask. The first aliquot drawn with a pipette still wet with water is diluted by 2.0%. Each size was chosen so that the error, on its own, fails a trial; the automated tests hold that for seeds 1 to 100. The sizes are parameters of the model, stored with the reason for each, and are not measurements. The feedback after a trial names each error, says which way it moves the result, and says that the lab makes it cost more than a real bench does. The primer says so too. No source was read for what these errors cost at a real bench.

**Rules that are not true to a bench.** Each is a simplification, chosen deliberately:

- A burette filled without conditioning stays 2.0% dilute through every refill, until it is conditioned. A real one is less diluted at each refill.
- The 0.60 mL bubble is more air than a real burette's tip holds, and it leaves evenly over the first 5 mL where a real one leaves at once. Both statements about a real burette are the project's own; no source was read for either.
- A wet pipette spoils only the first aliquot drawn with it, because drawing that aliquot wets the pipette with acid. A bubble that has escaped does not come back at a refill; conditioning empties the burette, so the fill after it traps a new one.
- The stopcock will not open until the flask holds 2 or 3 drops of indicator, and no indicator can be added once base has run in.
- The distilled water is free of carbon dioxide, and a flask takes at most 30 mL of it in a trial. A rinse is 10 mL, which can thin a very faint pink until it no longer shows; the bench and the primer say what to do then.
- The swirl and wait cannot fail: the model's flask is closed, so a pink that persists when it is added is never found faded 30 seconds later. The wait stands for the 30 seconds of a real bench, and the primer says so.
- Refilling the burette before each trial is taught and not forced. A trial run on what is left in the burette is an ordinary trial unless the burette runs out.
- A bubble run out into a flask that is then discarded, and a pipette wetted by an aliquot that is then discarded, leave the trials after them sound. So three trials can count although "Clear the tip" and "Rinse pipette with HCl" were never pressed. Conditioning cannot be got round in this way.

**What is not modelled.** Carbon dioxide taken up from the air during a trial (the acid in the flask starts with the carbon dioxide that water holds in equilibrium with outdoor air, the flask is then closed, and the base is free of carbonate); the slow fading of phenolphthalein's pink in strong base; activity corrections (the pH is that of an ideal solution, and no pH is shown to the learner); any temperature but 25 °C; the ethanol in the indicator solution; and parallax, which the primer explains.

**Conventions chosen.**

- The known solution is hydrochloric acid whose molarity is on its label and is taken as exact. The primer says that the acid was itself standardized.
- Molarities are written to four decimal places in every case. That is this lab's convention, not a significant-figure rule, and the primer says so.
- The endpoint is the first faint pink that lasts the 30-second swirl and wait.

**Known limits of the titration lab.**

- **No chemistry instructor and no chemist has reviewed the lab:** not the primer, the messages, the model or the hazard text.
- Eight points of the primer rest on no source that was read, and nobody has judged them one by one: that the faint pink of a real endpoint fades because the open flask takes up carbon dioxide, and that this is why the rule gives a time; that a sodium hydroxide solution which has stood is standardized again; touching a hanging part-drop to the flask wall; the definition of relative deviation; which way an escaping tip bubble moves the reading; the statements about a real bench given above; that a 10 mL rinse can thin a very faint pink until it no longer shows; and that a single drop's pink clears at once again after the stream's has begun to linger.
- Sentences written after the reviews have been read by no independent reader: those written to fix the last review's findings, and the labels, headings and button names written into the lab's screens. That includes everything written on 2026-10-06 when the page was put into tabs: the forty labels at the head of the primer's paragraphs, the choice of which terms are bold and which sentences are set off, the names of the tabs and of the primer's buttons, and the sentences shown where there is no run or where a stopcock was closed on leaving the Lab tab.

### The aqueous reactions lab

**What it teaches.** Ten fixed stations, each a test tube in which two reagents are mixed. The learner mixes the tube, observes it (by eye at every station, and at three stations with tools: wafting and red litmus paper, touch and a thermometer, a lit splint), records what was observed from fixed choices, and writes the reaction three ways: as a molecular equation, a complete ionic equation and a net ionic equation. Five stations give a precipitate, one gives no reaction, and the other four give ammonia, carbon dioxide, heat alone, and hydrogen with heat. The amounts are fixed; the learner does not choose them.

**The primer.** Eleven cards for a reader with no chemistry background, shown one at a time: ions in solution; solubility and the solubility table; precipitates; the three forms of an equation; working a station, step by step; spectator ions; gases and heat as signs of a reaction; when nothing happens; the limiting reagent; observing safely; safety. Five are marked to read first. The worked example of the equations card is a reaction that is at none of the stations, and so is the one that the card of worked steps takes from two bottles to three equations in eight labelled steps; automated tests hold every equation on both cards to balance in atoms and in charge. Each card lists its sources, and the primer quotes none of them.

**A station.** The tube's card shows the two reagents with what each looks like in its bottle, their amounts and their hazards, and after "Mix" a large drawing of the tube to explore: pointing at, focusing or tapping the surface of the liquid, the liquid or the bottom of the tube says in words what is seen there, and each of the three questions about what is seen opens once its part has been looked at. The tools each say what they show when used; touching the tube of station 8 says that it is hot and gives a caution, the thermometer there gives the rise and says that the tube is hot, and asking to smell a tube directly is refused with a caution on why. The observation is one choice for each thing the station's tools can show; its first question asks what solid is in the tube once any reaction is over, so that where a powder or a ribbon dissolves the answer is plainly "no solid". A station's notes, which say where the lab's account is simpler than a real tube and at three stations name the equation that is checked, are shown once the station is done and not before. Each equation is built from a palette of formulas offered for that station, which includes some that do not belong: a formula is chosen, placed as a reactant or a product, and given a coefficient and a state; "No reaction" can be chosen in its place. Nothing is typed. A solubility table, a table of the ions of the bench with their names and charges, and a calculator can be opened over the page.

**Two modes.** Guided shows each station's hints from the start, and offers what a correct observation holds in a list that is closed until the learner opens it. Challenge shows neither at a station until the learner has recorded an observation of their own there, right or not. The mode can be changed at any time, and no entry is labelled with a mode.

**How answers are checked.**

- An observation is correct when every choice is the one the lab's record expects for that station. The feedback names the questions to look at again and not the answers.
- An equation is correct when the substances on each side are those of the equation stored for that station and form, each state is one the substance accepts (ammonia may be a gas or dissolved), and the coefficients are in the stored proportion, so a doubled equation passes. The feedback says what kind of thing is wrong, the first of these that applies: an equation where nothing reacts, or "No reaction" where something does; an ion in a molecular equation; a dissolved compound not split into its ions in an ionic equation; a spectator ion left in a net ionic equation; the substances or their sides; a state; the balance. It never shows the stored equation.
- Attempts are unlimited and nothing is scored. A station is done when its observation and its three equations are correct, and the lab is complete when all ten are. The Record tab lists each entry as it was last checked and whether it is correct.

**The model.** For each station the lab works out, from the fixed amounts, how much of each ion and solid is in the tube, in whole billionths of a mole, and applies five rules in one pass: an insoluble pair of ions forms a solid; a hydrogen ion with carbonate gives carbon dioxide; ammonium with hydroxide gives ammonia; a hydrogen ion with hydroxide gives water and heat; and magnesium with a hydrogen ion gives hydrogen and heat. It respects the limiting reagent and leaves the coloured ions that are not used up in the liquid. Automated tests hold that its result at every station agrees with the record a correct observation is checked against, that every stored equation balances in atoms and in charge, and that no net ionic equation has a substance unchanged on both sides.

**Rules that are not true to a bench.** Each is a simplification, chosen deliberately:

- Every reaction goes to completion at once, and a precipitate takes every ion it can: the liquid over the blue-green and the pale green solids of stations 2 and 3 is colourless.
- At stations 2 and 3 the equation that is checked is the simple carbonate's. The solid that really forms is a basic carbonate; the station's note says so once the station is done.
- The temperature rise (about 40 °C at station 8, about 11 °C at station 10) is worked out as if no heat left the tube and the liquid were water. The thermometer's sentence and the primer say that it is idealised and that a real tube warms less. Heat is worked out for those two rules alone; the other three are given none.
- Ammonia is not shown bubbling out at station 6, where most of it stays dissolved.
- "Warm" and "hot" are the lab's own thresholds, a rise of 5 °C and of 25 °C, stored with the reason for each.
- The solubility table holds only the 23 pairs of ions that the ten stations can bring together. It says of any other pair that it is not listed. It lists ammonium with hydroxide as soluble, as its source's row for ammonium does; in the lab those two ions react by another rule, and that one cell is marked with a footnote's sign, with a line under the table that says the two make no solid but do react, giving ammonia, and names the primer's card on gases.

**Where the chemistry comes from.** The solubility of each pair, the forms of an equation and the place of magnesium above hydrogen are read from OpenStax Chemistry 2e; the enthalpies of formation from the NBS tables of 1982, read as a scan by eye; the colours, textures and odours from a set of pages on the reactions of metal ions, the same book, one page of Chemguide, a university's instruction sheet and four Wikipedia pages; the molar masses from PubChem; and the hazards from a supplier's safety data sheets (see Lab safety). Each station's Sources control lists what its values rest on. The research record, `docs/research/phase-4/sources/README.md`, says how each kind of value was read and what could not be sourced: among these, the hex value each colour is drawn in, the name "orange" for the mixed liquid of station 5, and the colours of the fresh precipitates at stations 2 and 3, which are read from descriptions of the dried solids.

**How the lab was checked.**

- **By automated tests.** The acceptance list of the design spec (section 8.4): each station's result against its expected record; the limiting reagent at stations 1, 7, 9 and 10; the two temperature rises; every stored equation's balance; the equation check with doubled coefficients and with a substance that does not belong; and the description of what is seen at every station. Also: that every state a learner can reach in 60 scripted random walks leaves the lab able to be completed and passes the stored format; that every equation of every station can be built with the page's builder; and the page's markup and wording.
- **By script in a browser.** The checks listed under "The aqueous reactions lab: accessibility" above.
- **By one reviewer that did not build it, which was not a person.** The lab was written and checked by AI coding assistants. On 2026-10-07, at the close of the phase, another AI coding assistant that had not built the lab reviewed its screens, its wording, its primer and its code, and ran it in a browser as described under accessibility, above. It changed twelve things, among them: a station's notes were shown as soon as the observation was correct, and three of them give an equation that was still to be entered, the control station's outright, so they are now shown when the station is done; the primer's card on the case where nothing reacts said that with no acid there is no gas, which is not so at station 6; the primer described the solubility table as a list when the page draws a grid; the question about a solid could be read as asking what went in; an open panel was left outside a window made smaller; and a completed lab said the same sentence four times on a station. The record of the build lists every finding, what was done, and what was left. No person has reviewed the lab: no chemistry instructor, no chemist, and no reader of any kind from outside the project.

**Known limits of the aqueous reactions lab.**

- **No chemistry instructor and no chemist has reviewed the lab:** not the primer, the stations' hints and notes, the feedback, the model or the hazard text.
- The record of sources says what each page was read to print. Only one of those pages is kept, so the build cannot check the rest again.

- At stations 2 and 3 the palette offers the metal's hydroxide as a formula that does not belong, and the solubility table has no row for that pair, so the table cannot rule it out; the station's own note, shown when the station is done, says that the solid that really forms does hold hydroxide.
- The palette at each station holds a few formulas that do not belong; how good they are as wrong answers has been judged by nobody.
- No learner has used the lab.

### The flame test lab

**What it teaches.** Six stations, one for each of six 0.1 M solutions, each the chloride of a different metal: barium, calcium, copper, potassium, sodium and strontium. At a station the learner cleans a wire loop, dips it in the solution, holds it in a burner's flame, looks at the flame, looks at the flame's light through a hand spectroscope with a scale from 400 to 780 nm, and records three things from fixed choices: the colour of the flame, where on the scale the brightest thing is (one of six ranges), and whether that thing is a sharp line or a broad band. There is no unknown to identify. When the six are done, one closing question asks what it is that gives each flame its colour, all six bottles being chlorides. The point the lab is built to make is that a flame's colour is not simply the colour of the atom's strongest lines: for calcium, strontium, barium and copper most of the light is a molecule's, formed in the flame, and shows as bands.

**The primer.** Six cards, shown one at a time: what a flame test is; light from atoms; molecules in a flame; the loop; the spectroscope and its scale; safety. Three are marked to read first. Each card lists its sources, and the primer quotes none of them.

**The technique that is checked is a clean loop.** A loop carries what it was dipped in until it is cleaned, and the loop the lab starts with has been handled and carries a trace of sodium. Whatever is on the loop shows in the flame and on the strip. An observation made with a loop that carried anything but the station's own solution does not count, whatever was recorded: the feedback says so and says what the loop carried, and the station is done again. In Guided a warning comes before such a loop is dipped, with a way back and a way on.

**Two modes.** Guided has hints at each station and what a correct observation holds, each in a list that is closed until the learner opens it, and gives the warning. Challenge gives no warning, and shows neither the hints nor the expected observation at a station until the learner has recorded an observation there that counts. The mode can be changed at any time.

**How answers are checked.**

- An observation that counts is correct when its three choices are the ones the lab's record gives for that salt: the flame's stored colour, the range that the salt's highest-ranked line or band lies in, and which of the two that is. The feedback names the questions to look at again and not the answers.
- The closing question has one right choice of five. A wrong one is told why it cannot be the answer, from what was seen at the bench, and not what the answer is.
- Attempts are unlimited and nothing is scored. A station is done when its observation was made with a clean loop and is correct, and the lab is complete when all six are done and the closing question is answered rightly. The Record tab lists what was last recorded at each station and whether it counted and is correct.

**Which line or band is "the brightest" follows what instruments recorded, not the eye.** No table of measured intensities of these flames was found. Each salt's lines and bands are ranked from what its sources say or draw: for calcium and strontium, the taller peak in emission spectra drawn in a paper of 1975, which are not corrected for the instrument or for the eye; for barium, what two sources say of its green band; for copper, what a dissertation's reprinted paper says of firework mixtures that hold a source of chlorine; for sodium and potassium, the relative intensity that the NIST database lists, which NIST calls qualitative. An eye at a real hand spectroscope may well disagree: it is far more sensitive to orange than to deep red, so it may find strontium's orange band the brighter where the lab says the red, and potassium's deep red pair near 766 nm, which the lab ranks first, lies at the edge of what an eye sees at all. The primer's card on the spectroscope says that brightness here is the instruments', and each line and band says in words how bright it is beside the others, so no answer depends on judging brightness from the screen.

**Rules that are not true to a bench.** Each is a simplification, chosen deliberately and stored with its reason:

- The trace of sodium on a handled loop shows as fully as a dip in the sodium chloride solution. A real trace is far smaller and often gives a brief, faint yellow.
- The loop is cleaned in one step, and a loop that is not clean does not spoil the solution it is dipped in.
- With sodium on the loop the flame is sodium's yellow alone, which has a source. With several salts and no sodium the flame is called by the colour of each and drawn in each side by side, and on the strip every salt's lines and bands are shown as that salt alone gives them: the model does not weigh one salt's light against another's.
- Two lines of one salt closer than 5 nm are shown as one line, as a hand spectroscope would show them. The 5 nm is the lab's figure, not a measurement of any instrument.
- A band whose sources give only where it is brightest is drawn 5 nm wider on each side, and says that its limits are not given.
- The strip shows the lines and bands that the sources place on the scale, and no others. Where a source names more light and gives no place for it (the bands of SrCl and of copper oxide), or where bands are spread over most of the scale or lie inside another (BaO and BaCl), the station says so in a sentence once it is done.
- The colours on the screen are approximate. The flame is drawn in a colour chosen by the project for the colour its source names. A line or a band is drawn in an approximation of the colour of its wavelength, and beyond 740 nm in the colour of 740 nm, so that potassium's deep red line can be found on a screen. A violet, a blue or a deep red that would be too dim against the strip's black is drawn paler, so that it can be found. How light a line or a band looks on a screen follows its colour as well as its rank: calcium's green band, ranked second, is lighter on a screen than its orange-red band, ranked first. Which is the brightest is in the words of each callout.
- A sharp line is drawn the strip's whole height and a broad band a little shorter, so that the two are told apart. That is the picture's way and not a spectroscope's, and the primer says so.
- The burner is no particular burner. The sources' flames were of hydrogen and oxygen in argon, of air and acetylene, of a gas burner in 1860, and of burning firework mixtures.

**Where the chemistry comes from.** The wavelengths of the atomic lines are the digits of the NIST Atomic Spectra Database, from tables saved in the repository; the build holds every stored line to the table of its own metal. The bands of CaOH, SrOH and BaOH are from a paper of 1975, read as a scanned copy in a university's repository, pages and figures by eye, and from the abstracts in English of two papers of 1979 whose text was not read; those of CuCl and CuOH from a doctoral dissertation on firework flames. The colours of the flames are from a page of Chemguide for five of the six and from an open textbook on qualitative analysis for sodium's, with that textbook and a paper as second sources. That a handled loop carries sodium, that sodium's yellow hides other colours, and the answer to the closing question rest on a university's page, a college's pre-lab page and the paper of Kirchhoff and Bunsen of 1860, read in an abridged translation. Each station's Sources control lists what its values rest on, once the station is done. The research record, `docs/research/flame/README.md`, says how each kind of value was read, what could not be sourced, and what a chemist should look at first: the ranks; copper, where a green band may in truth outshine the blue; barium, whose broad green band is given to BaOH though the solution is a chloride; and ranges taken from abstracts that may be the windows of a measurement and not the edges of a band.

**How the lab was checked.**

- **By automated tests.** The acceptance list of the design spec (section 15.5): that a clean loop gives each salt its stored flame and its stored lines and bands and nothing else; that a loop that was not cleaned gives those of everything on it, with sodium's yellow where sodium is on it, that an observation made so does not count and names its cause, and that Guided warns before the dip; that every stored line or band has a source and every atomic line the saved table's digits; that from four hundred actions taken as they come the lab can still be completed, and the stored record survives a round trip; that Challenge is handed nothing of what is expected before an observation that counts; and the descriptions in words of the flame and of the strip for every salt. Also the page's markup and wording, and where each line's and band's button lies.
- **By script in a browser.** The checks listed under "The flame test lab: accessibility" above.
- **By a review, on 2026-10-09,** of the whole lab by an AI coding assistant that had not built it: in a browser as said above, and by reading every sentence a learner is shown against the lab's record and the research record's account of its sources. It rewrote what the lab said of the periodic table's cards (next paragraph) and five sentences of the primer.
- **By nobody else.** The record, the engine, the primer, the wording and the screens were written, checked and reviewed by AI coding assistants.

**What the lab says of the periodic table's cards.** As first written, the notes of the calcium, strontium, barium and copper stations, the primer and the closing question's feedback said that those flames are not the colour of the lines on the element's card. The cards do not bear that out: six of the nine lines on calcium's card are orange or red and its flame is orange-red, and three of the six on strontium's are red and its flame is red. Since the review each says only that the card shows lines of the atom alone, and that what colours the flame is mostly the molecule's light, which the card does not show.

**Known limits of the flame test lab.**

- **No chemistry instructor and no chemist has reviewed the lab:** not the record, the ranks, the notes, the primer, the feedback, the closing question or the hazard text. The scanned paper was read by one reader, by eye.
- Every source but one saved table was read and is not kept, so the build cannot check the record against the pages again. It holds the record to the research record's account of what each page prints.
- The lab's ranking can differ from what a learner sees at a real spectroscope, as said above; a learner who has done the real experiment may have been taught another answer for strontium or potassium.
- The hints are the same five at every station, behind a list that is closed until it is opened.
- **The primer gives one of the three answers.** In both modes its cards say which two of the six flames show lines and which four are mostly bands, and that sodium's flame is yellow. A learner who has read them has, before the bench, the answer to "a sharp line or a broad band" at every station and one of the six colours. That is the design's choice, and it is why the search for what Challenge keeps back left the primer out. A station that is done also lists, among its sources, a paper whose title holds the formulas of two of the molecules.
- The answer to the closing question is the metal, and a learner who chooses the chloride is told that what is the same in all six bottles cannot be what makes the flames differ. At the copper station the brightest band is given to a molecule that holds chlorine; the lab does not say that the chloride takes part in that flame's light.
- No learner has used the lab.

### The mechanism timeline

**What it teaches.** What happens, molecule by molecule, when sodium hydrogen carbonate (baking soda) is added to hydrochloric acid and fizzes. The learner moves a timeline through seven chapters: the beaker before; the acid as it is before mixing, as hydronium ions and chloride ions; the solid dissolving into sodium ions and hydrogen carbonate ions; a hydronium ion giving a proton to a hydrogen carbonate ion, which makes carbonic acid; carbonic acid splitting into water and carbon dioxide, with a water molecule passing a proton along; the carbon dioxide leaving as gas; and the beaker afterwards. One unit of the solid and one of the acid stand for all of them: fourteen atoms, followed through the whole change.

**What a learner does.** Plays the timeline, steps through it with Previous and Next, or moves a slider to any point of it. The scene is drawn in 3D and can be turned and zoomed. Pointing at a molecule or an ion, or tapping it, names it, and a list beside the scene gives the same names with which of them take part in the chapter. Each chapter has a short text and a longer one that is closed until it is opened; the texts say what an ion, a proton, a lone pair and a double bond are where each word is first needed. A panel that floats over the page draws free energy and enthalpy along the timeline and says what each step changes.

**Two modes.** Guided asks nothing. Challenge stops before each of the four steps and asks what happens next, with four answers of which one is right. One press answers. The feedback says whether the answer is right and why, and the chapter's text follows. Until a question is answered the page keeps back the name of its step, and until every question is answered the energy panel gives its figures without the notes, the working and the sources that would answer some of them. The first answer to each question is the one kept, until the learner presses "Start again", which asks first. Nothing is scored, and when the end is reached the page says how many of the predictions were right. The timeline is completed when its end has been reached, in either mode, by playing or by moving the slider there.

**How answers are checked.** Each question and its four answers are in the timeline's record, with which one is right and a sentence of feedback for each. The stored progress holds which answer was chosen, and whether it was right is worked out from the record each time. Automated tests hold that each question has exactly one right answer.

**What is drawn, and what is not.**

- The molecules and the polyatomic ions have the shapes of the explorer's library: the experimental structure for water and carbon dioxide, and a computed conformer for the hydronium ion, the hydrogen carbonate ion and carbonic acid.
- Every ball is at one scale. The atoms of a molecule are balls a quarter of their van der Waals radius, as in the explorer's ball and stick view, and the sodium ion and the chloride ion are balls a quarter of their ionic radius, with the symbol and charge written beside them. So a sodium ion is drawn a little smaller than a hydrogen atom's ball, which is what the two radii give. A line under the scene says that every ball is drawn much smaller than the atom or ion it stands for, so that the bonds can be seen.
- **The solid is not drawn before it is added.** "Before mixing" shows the acid alone: a hydronium ion, a chloride ion and one water molecule. The unit of the solid comes into the picture in step 1, and until its two ions have moved apart it has one name, "unit of solid sodium hydrogen carbonate", and no charge written on its sodium, so that the page does not answer the first question.
- **A molecule or an ion is named from its bonds as they are.** While a proton crosses, the hydronium ion is called a hydronium ion until the proton is half-way, and a water molecule from then on.
- The water that fills the beaker is not drawn, nor the water that surrounds each ion. One water molecule is in the scene from the start, because it takes part in step 3. The first chapter's text says so.
- A bond that is forming, breaking or changing order is drawn as dashes, and the scene's description says how far along it is. Curved arrows show where each pair of electrons goes during the two steps in which bonds change: each starts at the edge of the atom a lone pair is on or at the middle of the bond a pair comes from, and ends at the middle of the bond the pair becomes or at the edge of the atom it stays on. They are flat marks on the picture, drawn over the atoms, and all the arrows of a step are shown together, though the pairs they stand for do not move one after another.
- The bubble is not drawn atom by atom, and the two bookends are a drawn beaker.

**The energies, and which of them are cited, derived or drawn.** All are for standard conditions at 25 °C, in kilojoules per mole. The panel says of each value which of these it is.

| Step | Free energy | Enthalpy | Barrier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Dissolving | +2.3, from cited formation values | +18.7, from cited formation values | none drawn |
| 2 Proton transfer | −19.9, from the cited acid constant of the carbonic acid molecule | counted with step 3 | no value; drawn, and labelled "not to scale" |
| 3 Decomposition | −16.4, derived: the cited total for steps 2 and 3, less step 2 | −7.6 for steps 2 and 3 together, from cited formation values | about 66, derived from how long a carbonic acid molecule lasts |
| 4 Gas escapes | −8.4, from cited formation values | +20.3, from cited formation values | none drawn |

The four steps sum to +31.4 for the enthalpy and −42.4 for the free energy, which is what the cited tables give for the whole change taken at once; an automated test holds the sums to that, and nothing was adjusted to reach it. The point the panel makes is that the change takes in heat and runs all the same, because the gas that escapes is far more spread out.

**No figure is read off a drawn curve.** Between the level a step starts at and the level it ends at, each curve is a smooth line that was chosen for the drawing, and so is the shape of a barrier. A cursor on the curves shows where the timeline is and gives no number. Every energy the page prints, and every one the scene's description gives, is a step's own whole change or a sum of whole steps. Automated tests hold that at a thousand points of the timeline.

**Rules that are not true to a real solution.** Each is a simplification, chosen deliberately:

- **Where the atoms are between and around the reacting molecules is Valence's own choreography.** It is not a computed or a measured path: how far apart two species are when they meet, where the water that passes the proton sits, the turn of one O–H group of carbonic acid before the proton moves, and the order in which things move were all placed by hand, from the library's shapes. The first chapter's longer text says so, step 3's says it of how the hydrogen crosses, and so do the panel's Sources control and the About page.
- **That one water molecule passes the hydrogen along in step 3 is the route drawn, not a finding.** No source was read for the path by which carbonic acid breaks up. The timeline draws one water molecule taking a hydrogen from one O–H group and giving one of its own to the other, in one move; more water molecules may take part in a real solution, and the step may not be one move.
- **The track is not linear in time.** A step that takes picoseconds has about as much room as one that takes seconds. Each step is labelled with its real timescale, and Play takes one minute for the whole track whatever the steps really take. Step 3 is labelled the slower of the two steps that change bonds; dissolving and the gas leaving, which change none, are labelled in seconds.
- **The acid constant of carbonic acid** was measured at 20.5 °C and is used at 25 °C with no correction. It is the constant of the carbonic acid molecule itself, 3.49, and not the 6.35 of textbooks, which counts dissolved carbon dioxide as the acid; a note in the panel says so.
- **The barrier of step 3** is worked out by the Eyring equation, with a transmission coefficient of one, from a lifetime that the source gives as about 60 milliseconds at a room temperature it does not state. A lifetime twice as long or half as long moves it by 1.7 kJ/mol.
- **The barrier of step 2 has no value.** It is drawn 10 kJ/mol high so that it can be seen, and labelled as not to scale.
- **Steps 1 and 4 are drawn with no barrier.** Neither is one move over a barrier.
- **The enthalpy curve shows steps 2 and 3 as one change,** because no heat of forming the carbonic acid molecule is cited. One has been published and was not read at its source; the research record says where.
- **The proton is drawn leaving a hydronium ion, H₃O⁺.** In the experiment the timescale of step 2 rests on, the proton came from another acid, made by a flash of light.

**Where the chemistry comes from.** The formation values are read from the NBS tables of 1982, as a scan, by eye. The acid constant of carbonic acid, how long it lasts, and the timescales of steps 2 and 3 are from one paper of 2016 in the Journal of Physical Chemistry B, read as the authors' manuscript: its text and tables, not its figures. Three constants are from NIST's listing of the 2022 CODATA values. The two ionic radii are Shannon's of 1976, checked through a transcription and not in the paper. The timescales of steps 1 and 4 ("seconds") have no source and are stored as everyday observations. The research record, `docs/research/phase-5/sources/README.md`, says how each kind of value was read, what is worked out from what, and what could not be sourced.

**How the timeline was checked.**

- **By automated tests.** The acceptance list of the design spec (section 9.3): that the scene is continuous at every chapter boundary; that each step's bonds broken and formed are the difference between the bond lists at its two ends; that the atoms and the total charge are the same at every boundary and every species there has its library record's bonds; that the energies sum as above; and that each chapter's link opens at its start. Also: that the scene at a position is the same whichever way it is reached; that no two atoms sit on or pass through each other; that every energy is worked out by the build from the cited values; the wording; the stored progress; the scene's description; the drawing against the model; and the page's markup.
- **By script in a browser.** The checks listed under "The mechanism timeline: accessibility" above.

## Lab safety

Valence makes no claim that what it shows about hazards meets any rule or standard for hazard communication. This section says what is shown, where it comes from, and what nobody has checked.

- **A supplement, not a replacement.** Valence is a simulation. It supplements bench work and does not replace it, and it does not replace safety training; the About page and the first paragraph of the primer's safety card say so. Nothing in it teaches the handling of real glassware or chemicals, and finishing a lab in Valence is no evidence that a learner can work safely at a bench.
- **What each bottle's hazard information classifies.** Each of the titration lab's three bottles has a "Hazards" disclosure: a heading that says whose hazards these are and in what form, under it the signal word and the hazard statements with their GHS codes, a note headed "At the strength used here", and the sources of both. The statements are those of one supplier's safety data sheets, Flinn Scientific's, for the concentrated or the pure substance, stored as the sheets print them: concentrated hydrochloric acid, 6 M to 12 M; solid sodium hydroxide; and solid phenolphthalein. All three give the signal word Danger. They are not the hazards of the dilute solutions on the lab's bench, and the heading over each, and the primer's safety card, say what they are for.
- **What each note rests on.** The note under the statements says what a safety data sheet gives for the solution at the strength on the bench, and has its own source, or two.
  - *Hydrochloric acid.* The label, 0.0850 to 0.1050 M, lies on both sides of the line between two of the same supplier's sheets: one for less than 0.1 M, which gives Warning, and one for 0.1 M to 2.4 M, which gives Danger and severe burns to the skin. The note gives both, says that the acid sits at the line between them, and says to handle it as the stricter sheet says. It does not call the acid mild.
  - *Sodium hydroxide.* The bench's solution, about 0.1 M, is under the same supplier's sheet for solutions of less than 0.3 M, which gives Warning and irritation of the skin and the eyes.
  - *Phenolphthalein.* The supplier's one sheet for a phenolphthalein solution is for a solution in alcohol alone, which is not the 0.5% solution in 50% alcohol the lab takes its bottle to be. So this note rests on another supplier's sheet, Ricca Chemical Company's, for a solution of that kind, and says that its sheet is a different supplier's. That the lab's bottle is such a solution is assumed.
- **The sheets are cited, not kept, so the build cannot re-check them.** Each sheet was read on the date its citation gives (2026-10-04 or 2026-10-05), and no copy is in the repository. The research record lists, row by row, what each sheet was read to print. The build holds the app's record to that list: every statement, in order, and the signal word. It cannot hold either of them to the sheets themselves. Whether the list is what the sheets print can be checked only by a person who reads the sheets, and a sheet that is revised later is not noticed. Two of the sheets, for the two solids, carry revision dates of 2014 and 2015; whether the supplier has later revisions was not looked into. None of the supplier's sheets says which regulation its classification is made under, and Valence does not say either.
- **Who has read the hazard text against the sheets.** The AI coding assistant that entered it, and then one independent AI check with the sheets loaded again. That check found that the first aid for a splash in the eyes was given shorter than the sheets give it, and made five other findings; what was written to fix them has been read by no independent reader. No safety officer, chemistry instructor or chemist has reviewed any of it.
- **First aid.** The notes and the primer's safety card say what the sheets say is to be worn, and what they say to do after a splash in the eyes or on the skin. The sheets give no length of time for the rinsing. The safety card says so, says that a laboratory sets its own rule and that a common one is 15 minutes at the eyewash, and says to follow the rule of the laboratory one is in. No source was read for the 15 minutes.
- **What is not covered.** The sheets' precautionary statements are not shown as such, and neither are two sentences on cancer listings that two of the sheets print outside their hazard statements. Nothing is said about first aid after breathing in or swallowing a solution, spills, storage, waste disposal, emergency equipment, or the choice of protective equipment for a particular laboratory. A laboratory's own safety data sheets, for the products it actually stocks, and its own rules take precedence over anything shown in Valence.
- **The exaggerated technique errors** of the titration lab (see Faculty and curriculum) are about the accuracy of a result. They are not about safety.
- **The mechanism timeline shows no hazard information.** It follows hydrochloric acid and sodium hydrogen carbonate at the scale of molecules and has no bench, no bottles and no amounts. Its first chapter calls the powder baking soda and says that the mixture fizzes; it says nothing of handling the acid.
